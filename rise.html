Case Study 02 · UX Design

RISE — Real Income, Steady Earnings

A design exploration into the first months after a first paycheck: how a banking experience might help young earners protect financial breathing room and make their income last until the next payday.

My roleIndividual contributor · UX Designer
Research approachSecondary research · Behavioural synthesis · Product design
Project statusSelf-directed concept · Not built or shipped
Portfolio focusProblem-solving · Evidence · Design decisions
01 · Problem

Getting the first job changes the pressure. It does not remove it.

RISE explores the financial transition after a first steady income. The problem framing starts with the hidden cost of looking for work, then follows the new responsibilities that arrive with the first payslip: rent, transport, food, family support and other commitments.

The wider context

The source deck records South African youth unemployment rising from 36.9% in 2015 to 47.4% by Q2 2026. The project asks what happens on the other side of that statistic: the first months of being paid.

The illustrative scenario

Thabo is a composite persona. His story uses 38 applications, 3 interviews and 1 job landed to illustrate the effort of reaching employment; these are not results from a single interviewed participant.

Problem statement: Young people entering employment may receive a regular income before they have the confidence or support to manage the responsibilities that come with it. How might a banking experience help them make their income last until the end of the month?
02 · Research method and thinking

I used three secondary-research signals to test whether the problem was structural.

This was a self-directed, secondary-research-led exploration. I selected three statistics to examine different dimensions of the same challenge: emotional pressure, confidence with digital financial skills and confidence about building a stable financial future. No primary user interviews were conducted.

EMOTIONAL STATE

75%

Of South Africans under 35 reported financial anxiety or stress, up 18% year-on-year.

Source listed in the deck: Debt Rescue Money Stress Tracker, 2026.

DIGITAL FINANCIAL SKILLS

39%

Reported confidence in their own digital financial skills.

Source listed in the deck: SA Social Media Landscape report.

FINANCIAL OUTLOOK

50%+

Reported not knowing how to build a stable financial future.

Source listed in the deck: Old Mutual Youth Generational Wealth Survey.

Research limitation: The figures are reproduced as attributed in the original deck. The source deck does not specify the publication year or full edition details for the latter two reports. Verify the exact editions and underlying populations before publishing these statistics. Skills and outlook are self-reported; income and cost-of-living data would strengthen the case.
03 · Synthesis and insights

The problem is a recurring pressure loop, not simply a budgeting task.

The three research signals informed a composite persona and a working behavioural hypothesis. Thabo Mahlangu represents a cautious first-job earner whose central need is financial breathing room: knowing what is safe to spend while keeping a buffer above zero.

Thabo · The Cautious Provider

  • 23 years old, Johannesburg, first job.
  • Illustrative income: R6,800 per month.
  • Checks his balance after payday, debit orders and larger purchases.
  • Splits money across transport, home, food and “do not touch”.
  • Wants to support home without losing financial progress.

What the synthesis suggests

  • Banks show what has happened, not necessarily what is about to happen.
  • Jargon can make financial questions feel embarrassing.
  • Surprise deductions weaken a sense of control.
  • Financial progress means ending the month with money remaining, not only accumulating savings.
The loop: payday arrives → family remittance claims a fixed share → little or no buffer remains → shame blocks help-seeking → no habit or safety net forms. The proposed product intervention sits around the point where shame can prevent someone from checking in or asking for help. Family remittance needs validation through interviews.
04 · Collaboration and influence

This was individual work, so the contribution is ownership rather than team facilitation.

RISE was a self-directed exploration and the source material identifies the role as Individual Contributor, UX Designer. I carried out the secondary research, developed the composite persona and behavioural synthesis, framed the design direction and translated it into interface concepts. There were no documented customer interviews, stakeholder workshops or external collaborators.

Work I owned

  • Selected and interpreted the secondary-research signals.
  • Developed the composite persona and financial pressure loop.
  • Defined the design objective and success measure.
  • Developed five design decisions and explored the interface.

What remains unvalidated

  • Whether the loop reflects the experiences of real first-job earners.
  • How family obligations vary across households.
  • Whether the proposed Buffer language and features feel useful and non-judgemental.
  • How irregular or gig income changes the experience.
05 · Design direction

Design for financial breathing room, not just account management.

The product direction focused on a specific outcome: helping a first-job earner understand what is safe to spend and protect a small financial buffer until the next payday. Five design decisions were mapped to barriers in the pressure loop.

01

Protect a Buffer

Make a protected buffer visible as a first-class financial object, not just another savings goal.

02

Make help approachable

Offer an Ask RISE quick-question channel so a user does not need to know the right financial terminology before asking.

03

Teach in context

Place progressive Money 101 lessons alongside everyday banking decisions rather than behind a separate literacy hub.

04

Understand the person

Start onboarding with employment stage, income and priorities, not only a dense compliance sequence.

05

Warn before the shortfall

Use a projected shortfall and a practical next action rather than relying only on fixed low-balance alerts.

Design principle: Reduce financial uncertainty without creating another restrictive banking experience. Keep the user in control, make guidance optional and focus support around moments of pressure.
06 · Solution and design decisions

Five design decisions turn the strategy into an experience.

The interface exploration connects the Buffer, contextual help, progressive learning, onboarding and predictive warnings into one banking concept. The work includes original notebook sketches, a user flow, high-fidelity screens and a visual system.

DECISION 01

Buffer as a first-class object

Surface the Buffer during onboarding and throughout the experience. It protects stability and should not compete with aspirational savings goals.

DECISIONS 02–03

Ask RISE + Money 101

Use suggested prompts to make questions easier to ask, and short lessons that appear near the financial decision a person is making.

DECISION 04

Context-first onboarding

Ask lightweight questions about employment stage, income and priorities so the experience can understand the transition before recommending support.

DECISION 05

Predictive Buffer warning

Warn when an action is likely to reduce the protected buffer, showing what may happen next and what the user could do to protect breathing room.

Trade-offs considered: protect the Buffer without locking money away; warn early without creating alert fatigue; offer guidance without undermining independence; defer complex automation until trust, income patterns and preferences can be tested.
07 · Success and impact

Success means the Buffer survives until the next payday.

RISE has not been built or shipped, so there are no measured user or business outcomes. The project defines a proposed north-star measure that tests the real-life outcome the concept is designed to support.

PRIMARYBuffer balance greater than zero the day before the next payslip.
SECONDARYApp opens on payday, as a signal that a checking-in habit may be forming.
REJECTEDSign-up rate alone, because account creation does not prove that financial pressure has changed.
North-star question: Does Thabo still have something in his Buffer the day before his next payslip lands? This is a proposed measure, not a result achieved by the concept.
08 · Lessons learned and next steps

The concept needs real first-job earners to challenge its assumptions.

The work suggests that financial support should respond to the moments when pressure builds, not only display balances after money has moved. It also highlights the limits of secondary research: the behavioural loop is useful for framing a design hypothesis, but it cannot establish how all young earners manage family support, irregular income or financial anxiety.

Lessons learned

  • Financial pressure should not be framed only as a lack of discipline or budgeting knowledge.
  • Secondary research can expose patterns, but self-reported measures and composite personas need primary validation.
  • A useful success measure should reflect a person’s financial reality, not just app engagement.
  • Predictive support must preserve choice and avoid making the app feel controlling.

Next steps

  • Interview first-job earners and validate the pressure loop, especially family remittance.
  • Stress-test the concept against irregular and gig income.
  • Test whether people understand and trust the Buffer and predictive warnings.
  • Usability-test Ask RISE, onboarding and everyday financial decisions.
  • Measure whether a buffer remains before the next income event.
Portfolio takeaway: I used secondary evidence and behavioural thinking to frame a focused financial problem, define a meaningful success measure and translate the strategy into a coherent product concept.

THE FIRST PAYCHEQUE · THABO'S STORY

Getting the job was only the beginning.

Follow Thabo from the effort of finding work to the financial pressure that arrives with his first steady income.

01Applying for jobs

Thabo spends evenings searching and applying for entry-level roles, hoping one opportunity will open a door.

02Printing his CV

He pays to use an internet cafe and print several copies of his CV, carefully checking each page before leaving.

03Buying data

With an interview approaching, he has to buy mobile data so he can join the Microsoft Teams meeting from home.

04The Teams interview

He finds a quiet spot, checks his connection and joins the Teams call, trying to make a strong impression despite the pressure.

05Job offer after many rejections

After many rejections and unanswered applications, the call finally comes through: Thabo has been offered the job. The relief and excitement are enormous. After so many setbacks, he finally has a chance to begin a new chapter.

06More responsibilities, less certainty

His salary now has to stretch across rent, transport, food, family support and other commitments. He is proud to be working, but overwhelmed and unsure how to organise his money or what he can safely spend.

Job adverts tell people what they will earn, but rarely account for the real costs of finding work: printing CVs, travelling to interviews and buying data to get online. For Thabo, getting the job was what mattered. He did not expect that, once he started earning, he would also have to figure out how to divide his salary between rent, transport, food, family support and other commitments - and make it last until the end of the month.

The transition into work brings new responsibilities before financial confidence has had time to catch up.

RESEARCH GOALHow might we help young people entering employment understand and manage their new financial responsibilities so they can make their income last until the end of the month?

PRODUCT THINKING · TRADE-OFFS

Protecting the Buffer without taking control away.

The design had to reduce financial uncertainty without creating another restrictive banking experience.

CHOICE 01

Safety vs. spending freedom

Trade-off: Locking money away could protect savings, but would make the Buffer feel inaccessible.

Decision: Make the Buffer visible and intentional, while keeping the user in control of transfers.

CHOICE 02

Early warnings vs. alert fatigue

Trade-off: Frequent alerts may catch problems early, but can create anxiety and be ignored.

Decision: Focus on a projected shortfall and pair the warning with a practical next action.

CHOICE 03

Guidance vs. independence

Trade-off: More advice can support confidence, but risks overwhelming or judging the user.

Decision: Offer contextual, optional guidance rather than prescriptive financial rules.

WHAT STAYS OUT OF THE MVP

Complex automation

Automated transfers and highly personalised recommendations are deferred until trust, income patterns and user preferences can be tested.

Design principle: make the risk visible, make the next step understandable, and leave the decision with the person.
