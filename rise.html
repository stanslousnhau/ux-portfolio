RISE — Real Income, Steady Earnings

A design exploration into the first months after a first paycheck: how a banking experience might help young earners protect financial breathing room and make their income last until the next payday.

My roleIndividual contributor · UX Designer
Research approachSecondary research · Behavioural synthesis · Product design
Project statusSelf-directed concept · Not built or shipped

3–4 MIN READ · EVIDENCE-LED CASE STUDY
01 · Problem

Getting the first job changes the pressure. It does not remove it.

RISE explores the transition after a first steady income. A payslip brings transport, food, household contributions and family support before a young earner may have the confidence or support to manage them. How might banking help income last until the next payday?

02 · Research method and thinking

Secondary research framed the problem; it did not replace user interviews.

I selected three signals covering emotional pressure, digital financial skills and confidence about building a stable future. Together, they challenged a purely individual or budgeting-skills explanation. No primary interviews were conducted.

03 · Synthesis and insights

The challenge is a pressure loop, not simply a budgeting task.

The signals informed Thabo, a composite first-job earner who wants to support home without losing financial progress. The synthesis suggested that banking often shows what has happened, not what may happen next. Jargon can make questions embarrassing; surprise deductions weaken control.

PAYDAY→FAMILY REMITTANCE→NO BUFFER→SHAME / NO HELP→NO SAFETY NET

Working hypothesis: The loop is inferred from secondary research and persona synthesis, not validated participant behaviour. Family remittance patterns need primary research.

04 · Collaboration and influence

This was individual concept work, with clear validation gaps.

I owned the secondary research, persona synthesis, problem framing, design direction and interface exploration. There were no documented customer interviews, stakeholder workshops or external collaborators. This is an individual design exploration, not a tested service or client delivery.

WHAT I OWNED

End-to-end concept

Evidence selection, composite persona, pressure-loop hypothesis, product decisions and success measure.

WHAT NEEDS VALIDATION

Real-life context

Family obligations, irregular income, the language of Buffer and whether support feels useful rather than judgemental.

05 · Design direction

Design for financial breathing room, not just account management.

The direction focused on helping a first-job earner understand what is safe to spend and protect a small buffer until the next income event. Five decisions map to the pressure loop.

01

Protect a Buffer

Make the safety margin visible as a first-class financial object.

02

Make help approachable

Ask RISE reduces the pressure to know the right financial terms.

03

Teach in context

Place Money 101 beside real decisions, not in a separate learning hub.

04

Understand the person

Use employment stage, income and priorities to shape onboarding.

05

Warn before a shortfall

Show a projected risk and a practical next action.

06 · Solution and design decisions

Five decisions connect the strategy to an everyday banking experience.

The concept combines Buffer visibility, contextual help, progressive learning, context-first onboarding and predictive warnings. The artefacts show the ideas moving from notebook exploration into a user flow and high-fidelity interface.

Design trade-offs

Protect the Buffer without locking money away; warn early without alert fatigue; offer guidance without undermining independence; and defer complex automation until trust, income patterns and preferences can be tested.

07 · Success and impact

Success means the Buffer survives until the next payday.

RISE has not been built or shipped, so there are no measured user or business outcomes. I defined success around the real-life result the concept is intended to support, rather than account creation or app activity alone.

PRIMARYBuffer balance above zero the day before the next payslip
SECONDARYPayday app opens as a possible check-in habit signal
NOT ENOUGHSign-up alone does not prove financial pressure has changed
08 · Lessons learned and next steps

Real first-job earners need to challenge the assumptions.

The work suggests financial support should respond when pressure builds, not only display balances after money moves. It also shows the limits of secondary research: a behavioural hypothesis cannot explain every household, income pattern or relationship with family support.

  • Interview first-job earners and validate the pressure loop, especially family remittance.
  • Test the concept against irregular and gig income.
  • Check whether people understand and trust Buffer warnings.
  • Usability-test Ask RISE, onboarding and everyday decisions.
  • Measure whether a buffer remains before the next income event.

My takeaway: A meaningful product metric should reflect a person’s financial reality, not just engagement.

THE FIRST PAYCHEQUE · THABO'S STORY

Getting the job was only the beginning.

Follow Thabo from the effort of finding work to the financial pressure that arrives with his first steady income.

01Applying for jobs

Thabo spends evenings searching and applying for entry-level roles, hoping one opportunity will open a door.

02Printing his CV

He pays to use an internet cafe and print several copies of his CV, carefully checking each page before leaving.

03Buying data

With an interview approaching, he has to buy mobile data so he can join the Microsoft Teams meeting from home.

04The Teams interview

He finds a quiet spot, checks his connection and joins the Teams call, trying to make a strong impression despite the pressure.

05Job offer after many rejections

After many rejections and unanswered applications, the call finally comes through: Thabo has been offered the job. The relief and excitement are enormous. After so many setbacks, he finally has a chance to begin a new chapter.

06More responsibilities, less certainty

His salary now has to stretch across rent, transport, food, family support and other commitments. He is proud to be working, but overwhelmed and unsure how to organise his money or what he can safely spend.

Job adverts tell people what they will earn, but rarely account for the real costs of finding work: printing CVs, travelling to interviews and buying data to get online. For Thabo, getting the job was what mattered. He did not expect that, once he started earning, he would also have to figure out how to divide his salary between rent, transport, food, family support and other commitments - and make it last until the end of the month.

The transition into work brings new responsibilities before financial confidence has had time to catch up.

RESEARCH GOALHow might we help young people entering employment understand and manage their new financial responsibilities so they can make their income last until the end of the month?

PRODUCT THINKING · TRADE-OFFS

Protecting the Buffer without taking control away.

The design had to reduce financial uncertainty without creating another restrictive banking experience.

CHOICE 01

Safety vs. spending freedom

Trade-off: Locking money away could protect savings, but would make the Buffer feel inaccessible.

Decision: Make the Buffer visible and intentional, while keeping the user in control of transfers.

CHOICE 02

Early warnings vs. alert fatigue

Trade-off: Frequent alerts may catch problems early, but can create anxiety and be ignored.

Decision: Focus on a projected shortfall and pair the warning with a practical next action.

CHOICE 03

Guidance vs. independence

Trade-off: More advice can support confidence, but risks overwhelming or judging the user.

Decision: Offer contextual, optional guidance rather than prescriptive financial rules.

WHAT STAYS OUT OF THE MVP

Complex automation

Automated transfers and highly personalised recommendations are deferred until trust, income patterns and user preferences can be tested.

Design principle: make the risk visible, make the next step understandable, and leave the decision with the person.
