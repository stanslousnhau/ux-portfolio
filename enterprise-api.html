Case Study 01 · UX Designer

Enterprise API Community of Practice

A discovery engagement into the human layer of an enterprise API ecosystem: why teams rebuild, what makes reuse difficult, and how a connected community could help.

UX ResearchStakeholder interviewsAffinity mappingService and systems thinking
Original research presentation rebuilt as a responsive website case study.
01 · Problem

The API Marketplace existed. Confidence in reuse did not.

The API Marketplace was already live, but leadership lacked structured evidence about how teams experienced it or why discovery, trust and reuse were breaking down. The brief was to investigate the human layer of the ecosystem and inform the design of a proposed API Community of Practice.

THE PLATFORM

A live API Marketplace

The technical platform existed; the unresolved question was whether teams could find, trust and reuse what was already available.

THE GAP

No structured evidence

Leadership suspected problems with collaboration, discoverability and reuse, but did not yet have a clear picture of where or why they occurred.

THE QUESTION

What makes teams build again instead of reuse?

The investigation focused on organisational conditions behind ownership, incentives, collaboration, discovery and reuse.

02 · Research method and thinking

A research process designed to surface behaviour, not just opinion.

I designed the interview protocol and stayed involved from research design through synthesis. The method moved from role-specific questioning to cross-functional interviews, affinity mapping and a maturity read for leadership.

01 · DESIGN

Protocol and objectives

Set four objectives: understand the ecosystem across business and technical roles; identify barriers to collaboration, discovery, reuse and knowledge-sharing; establish qualitative maturity indicators; and assess readiness for a Community of Practice.

02 · LISTEN

16 stakeholder interviews

Interviews covered solution architects, subject matter experts, business analysts, developers, UX designers, Heads of APIs and executives/business stakeholders. Each session ran 30–45 minutes and was recorded with consent for analysis.

03 · SYNTHESISE

Affinity mapping and themes

Transcript observations were broken into fragments, clustered bottom-up, labelled as patterns and prioritised by frequency, intensity and business impact.

My contribution: I designed the full interview protocol, including consent and opening framing, a five-section question flow, scenario-based prompts and a separate executive track. I led 9 of the 16 interviews.
03 · Synthesis and insights

Six themes showed that the barriers were deeper than the platform.

I broke interview transcripts into single observations, clustered them by underlying behaviour rather than interview question, checked patterns across stakeholder groups and prioritised them by frequency, intensity and business impact. The process produced 20 insights and six themes.

01

Discovery is people-dependent

Teams found APIs through tribal knowledge and personal networks, not through the platform. Confidence in what already existed was low.

02

Duplication is normalised

Building something new felt lower-risk than finding, assessing and trusting someone else’s work.

03

Deep-rooted habits

Established ways of working outweighed the case for adopting a shared model.

04

Fragmented ownership

Unclear accountability for shared capabilities discouraged reuse and made governance feel like friction.

05

Governance felt like a gate

Processes were associated with delay, pushing teams towards workarounds.

06

Incentives do not reward reuse

Recognition and delivery pressure favoured shipping something new over contributing to shared capability.

Central insight: API reuse was not only a discoverability or tooling problem. Trust, ownership, habits and incentives shaped whether teams were willing to reuse what already existed.
04 · Collaboration and influence

The evidence became stronger when the team interpreted it together.

This was a collaborative discovery engagement within a seven-person design team. My responsibility was to keep the research connected from protocol through interviews and synthesis, while making space for the wider team and bank stakeholders to challenge and interpret the patterns.

What I owned

  • Designed the interview protocol and role-specific tracks.
  • Led 9 of the 16 stakeholder interviews.
  • Facilitated the affinity-mapping exercise.
  • Facilitated the ideation session.

What we shared

  • Reviewed emerging patterns as a seven-person design team.
  • Worked with bank stakeholders on a shared affinity and ideation board.
  • Tested what each pattern meant before treating it as a finding.
  • Used behaviour-change questions to move from insight to ideas.
Influence: The session helped create shared ownership of the Community of Practice direction. The ideas were shaped with the people who would be involved in running it, rather than simply handed over by the design team.
05 · Design direction

Move from a platform-centred fix to an ecosystem that can adapt.

The research challenged the assumption that better tooling alone would resolve reuse. The deeper barriers were cultural and behavioural, so the recommendation was to rethink the Community of Practice operating model itself.

Design direction: make ownership visible, reduce the perceived risk of trusting shared APIs, and recognise reuse and contribution as valued work.
06 · Solution and design decisions

The output was a set of strategic recommendations, not a new interface.

The project informed the proposed Community of Practice through three practical implications and a broader operating-model reframe. These recommendations were delivered as an evidence base for the Community of Practice design; they should not be read as implemented changes.

01 · DISCOVERY

Make people part of discovery

Make API owners and named contacts findable alongside each API, because teams relied on trusted people more than platform listings.

02 · TRUST

Make reuse feel safer

Lower the effort and uncertainty involved in finding, assessing and trusting a shared API so reuse can become the easier default.

03 · INCENTIVES

Give reuse a payoff

Recognise reuse and contribution as valued work, rather than treating them as extra process alongside delivery.

Operating-model decision: move away from fixed processes and central control towards self-organising parts, distributed ownership, continuous adaptation and incentives that support healthy reuse behaviours.
07 · Success and impact

Success was a defensible evidence base and a clearer direction for leadership.

The research themes, maturity read and machine-to-organism reframe were delivered to leadership as the evidence base for the Community of Practice design. The documented impact is the quality and usefulness of the research output, not a measured increase in API reuse.

DELIVERED

Traceable research evidence

Each theme was mapped to multiple stakeholder voices rather than relying on isolated anecdotes.

DELIVERED

A reframed problem

The challenge was articulated as a culture, incentives and behaviour issue as much as a platform issue.

DELIVERED

Shared language

The machine-versus-organism metaphor gave leadership a concise way to discuss operating-model trade-offs.

What was not measured: The source material does not report a post-project change in API discovery time, reuse rate, duplicate API creation or developer satisfaction. Those would be useful future measures, not achieved results.
08 · Lessons learned and next steps

Stay close to the evidence, and validate the operating model before reaching for tooling.

The strongest lesson from this engagement was the value of continuity: staying involved from protocol design through interviews and synthesis made it easier to notice subtle patterns around incentives and ownership before they were lost in hand-offs.

Lessons learned

  • Enterprise problems can look technical while being shaped by culture, incentives and behaviour.
  • Interview design should make it possible to surface concrete examples, not just opinions.
  • Affinity mapping is stronger when the team challenges what a pattern means before calling it a finding.
  • Staying close to the work from protocol through synthesis protects the thread between evidence and recommendation.

Next steps to validate

  • Test the proposed organism model with the people expected to participate in the Community of Practice.
  • Clarify ownership, named contacts and contribution expectations for shared APIs.
  • Establish baselines for API discovery, reuse and duplicate creation.
  • Check whether recognition and governance changes make reuse easier in practice.

These are suggested validation steps; the original slides do not document them as completed work.

Portfolio takeaway: I can design and facilitate enterprise research, connect stakeholder evidence into defensible themes and help a team reframe a complex organisational challenge.
