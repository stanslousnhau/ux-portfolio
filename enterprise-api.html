Enterprise API Ecosystem Research

I researched why teams struggled to find and reuse existing APIs, then synthesised the findings into practical recommendations for improving discovery and trust.

RoleUX Designer
Team7 designers
ContextTier-1 African bank · NDA

01 / OVERVIEW
Summary

Research revealed that API reuse was a people and operating-model problem, not just a search problem.

Within a large African banking environment, I helped investigate why teams rebuilt capabilities instead of reusing APIs already available. I designed the interview protocol, independently led 9 of 16 stakeholder interviews, contributed to synthesis and helped translate the findings into six cross-cutting themes and recommendations.

Scope and evidence: Qualitative discovery under NDA; findings informed recommendations, not a measured production outcome.

02 / THE PROBLEM
Problem

Why were teams rebuilding APIs instead of reusing existing ones?

The API Marketplace existed, but people still relied on personal networks to find capabilities. Unclear ownership, uncertainty about support and delivery pressure made reuse feel risky or slower than rebuilding.

03 / PROBLEM FRAMING
Issue tree

Break the ecosystem challenge into four areas to investigate.

The tree structures the research problem into connected questions. The branches are lines of inquiry, not claims that every factor was confirmed.

CORE QUESTION

What influences API ownership, collaboration, discovery and reuse across the enterprise?

01 · Ownership

  • Who is responsible for APIs across their lifecycle?
  • How clearly are responsibilities and accountability understood?
  • Where might ownership become fragmented?

02 · Incentives and culture

  • What motivates teams to create or reuse APIs?
  • How do team priorities influence reuse?
  • What cultural factors may discourage sharing?

03 · Collaboration

  • How do teams coordinate across boundaries?
  • How are dependencies and handoffs managed?
  • Where might coordination break down?

04 · Discovery and reuse

  • How do teams find existing APIs?
  • How do they assess suitability and support?
  • What makes reuse easier or harder than rebuilding?
Research outcome

Identify recurring organisational and ecosystem factors, then use the evidence to shape practical recommendations.

RESEARCH HYPOTHESIS

Challenges with API adoption and reuse may be influenced not only by technical factors, but also by unclear ownership, misaligned incentives, team culture and limited cross-team collaboration.

Research goal: Investigate how organisational structures, team behaviours and collaboration practices influence API ownership, discovery and reuse, and identify opportunities to improve the enterprise API ecosystem.

05 / RESEARCH APPROACH
Protocol design

Every question had a job: understand the ecosystem, expose barriers and shape the CoP.

I structured the protocol to move from participants’ roles and everyday experience into specific behaviours, organisational conditions and future possibilities. The sequence helped establish context before exploring sensitive friction, while scenario prompts made it easier to discuss what teams actually do.

01 · Opening and context

Build trust and understand each person’s position

Question focus: role, relationship to APIs, and how participants currently discover or interact with them.

Objective: understand how the API ecosystem is experienced across product, platform, architecture, engineering, delivery and business teams.

My thinking: establish psychological safety and a shared baseline before asking people to describe problems or workarounds.

02 · Current state

Find the friction in real workflows

Question focus: what works, where discovery becomes difficult, when teams reinvent existing capabilities, and what happens when an API cannot be found or trusted.

Objective: identify opportunities and barriers related to collaboration, discoverability, reuse and knowledge-sharing.

My thinking: ask about recent examples rather than general satisfaction, so the discussion surfaces concrete actions, dependencies and workarounds.

03 · Delivery behaviour

Understand what teams do when conditions change

Question focus: scenario-based prompts about changes to a shared API, governance, ownership, handoffs and reuse decisions.

Objective: identify practical barriers to reuse and assess how ready the organisation is for broader ecosystem enablement.

My thinking: scenarios reveal how people respond when responsibilities, dependencies or API contracts shift, rather than relying only on what they say they would do.

04 · Culture and readiness

Explore the human conditions behind sharing

Question focus: knowledge-sharing, incentives, team priorities, cross-team relationships and appetite for a Community of Practice.

Objective: assess organisational readiness for an API Community of Practice and identify the conditions that could support participation.

My thinking: API reuse depends on more than finding documentation. I wanted to understand whether people have the motivation, trust and connections to share knowledge across team boundaries.

05 · Future state and executive track

Connect everyday experience to strategic change

Question focus: the desired two-year future, the biggest lever for improvement, leadership’s role, strategic risks and investment priorities.

Objective: establish qualitative indicators of ecosystem maturity to inform the CoP’s design, operating model and governance approach.

My thinking: keep the themes consistent across roles, but adjust the altitude. Practitioners described lived experience; executives considered system-level conditions and the ability to enable change.

Why this sequence mattered

It moved from context to current behaviour, then to collaboration and culture, before asking participants to imagine change. This reduced the risk of leading people toward a preferred solution too early.

06 / SYNTHESIS AND RECOMMENDATIONS
Key decisions and trade-offs

Move from a machine that is not working to an organism that can learn, adapt and connect.

The recommendation was not to discard the API Marketplace. It was to recognise that a platform alone could not resolve the people and operating-model barriers around it. The machine-to-organism metaphor reframed the ecosystem from a fixed, centrally controlled mechanism into a connected living system shaped by relationships, learning and shared ownership.

Strategic reframe

From machine to organism

A machine model emphasises tools, control and predictable inputs. The organism model recognises that API adoption also depends on trust, incentives, shared learning and the ability of teams to adapt together.

Community and identity

Create belonging, not just a directory

Build a Community of Practice that gives API practitioners a shared identity and a reason to participate. People should be able to recognise themselves as part of a wider community, exchange experience and learn from peers across organisational boundaries.

Self-organising capability

Enable interaction and learning

Design the community to grow through member contribution, peer support and shared problem-solving, rather than depending entirely on central coordination. Create a capability where different users can ask questions, share patterns, discover expertise and learn more about APIs.

The trade-off

Governance and consistency still matter in a banking environment. The opportunity is to pair clear standards and accountability with space for the community to organise around real needs. Too much central control risks reproducing the machine model; too little structure can weaken trust, quality and ownership.

Affinity mapping

Make sense of the system together

I facilitated the affinity mapping exercise with the wider team, clustering observations bottom-up by behaviour rather than by interview question. This helped connect individual workarounds to recurring patterns and gave the team a shared basis for interpreting what the ecosystem needed.

Ideation session

Turn the insight into a different operating model

The ideation session used the emerging themes to explore how the bank could move beyond a tool-led response. The machine-to-organism reframe helped the team consider community, shared identity, self-organisation and learning as connected parts of the proposed capability.

07 / OUTCOME AND LEARNINGS
Outcome / learnings

The research reframed API enablement as a living ecosystem, not a standalone tool.

The work connected interview evidence and six cross-cutting themes to a strategic direction: pair the existing platform with a community that builds identity, belonging, peer learning and shared responsibility. The organism metaphor gave leadership a way to discuss the human and organisational conditions needed for API reuse.

What I learned

When a system is not working, improving its interface may not be enough. The more useful design question can be how people connect, learn, take ownership and adapt together around the system.

08 / NEXT STEPS
Next steps

Build the conditions for an API community to behave like an organism.

  • Co-design the community with its members. Define a shared purpose, identity and participation model with API consumers, owners, architects, developers and business stakeholders.
  • Create the conditions for self-organisation. Establish lightweight roles, contribution patterns and community rituals that let members surface needs and organise around them, supported by clear guardrails.
  • Design for interaction and learning. Explore capabilities for peer questions, reusable examples, knowledge exchange, finding expertise and learning about APIs across teams.
  • Connect the community to the existing Marketplace. Make it easier to move between finding an API, understanding its context and owner, and getting help from people who have used it.
  • Pilot and learn. Start with a focused group and real API tasks, then evaluate participation, time to find expertise, confidence in reuse, knowledge contribution and signals of duplicated effort.
